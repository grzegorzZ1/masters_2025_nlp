1. How does the image of Ukraine change in his speeches from 2000 to 2024?


A very clear process of radicalization should emerge, although it is not linear.

In the first phase, roughly until 2004, Ukraine is primarily portrayed as a neighbor, a partner, and a state closely connected with Russia economically, historically, and culturally. The first major turning point comes in 2004. The Orange Revolution becomes, for Putin, not merely a Ukrainian event, but an example of Western political interference and a technology of regime change.

Between 2008 and 2013, there is a growing tendency to question Ukraine’s full political agency. The view emerges that Ukraine is not a state like other states and that Russians and Ukrainians essentially constitute one people.

2014 marks the second fundamental turning point. Ukraine begins to be described simultaneously through two seemingly contradictory frames: as part of a common Russian-Ukrainian historical space and as a state taken over by nationalists and the West. Within this construction, Ukrainians remain brothers, while the Ukrainian state and its elites are increasingly delegitimized. The concepts of Novorossiya, the protection of Russian-speaking populations, and the portrayal of the Maidan as a Western-backed coup also appear.

2021–2022 brings a transition from paternalism to an effective questioning of Ukraine’s separate statehood. After 24 February 2022, Ukraine is portrayed primarily as an instrument of the West, a NATO foothold, and an anti-Russia, while the war, formally fought against Ukraine, is presented in Putin’s discourse primarily as a war against the West. At the same time, the distinction between the brotherly Ukrainian people and the allegedly imposed regime is maintained.

Four phases:
partnership → growing paternalism → Ukraine taken over by the West → questioning of separate political agency/statehood.

A model that merely states that Putin has always been anti-Ukrainian should receive a low score, because it would fail to capture the fundamental dynamics of this evolution.


2. When do themes of sovereignty begin to dominate foreign policy discourse?


Sovereignty and the independence of foreign policy are already present around 2000, in connection with Russia’s reaction to the experiences of the 1990s, Kosovo, and U.S. dominance.

Between 2004 and 2007, their intensity increases considerably. The color revolutions and NATO enlargement reinforce the belief that the state must be resistant to external interference.

Another major increase occurs after 2012, when sovereignty becomes a political slogan. After 2014, sovereignty becomes one of the central concepts organizing the Kremlin’s entire approach to international politics.

Phases:

presence (2000–2004) → strong increase (2004–2007) → even greater intensity after 2012–2014


3. At what point does Putin start talking about a multipolar world?


The concept of multipolarity was part of Russian foreign policy even before Putin, particularly under Yevgeny Primakov. Analyses from as early as 2000 already referred to Russia’s pursuit of a multipolar world as a counterweight to American unipolarity.

However, 2007 is the moment when multipolarity becomes one of the most recognizable elements of Putin’s personal political message. In Munich, he openly argues that the unipolar model is both unacceptable and impossible, and that the development of new centers of power will strengthen multipolarity.

During the 2010s, multipolarity evolves from a demand to limit U.S. dominance into a broader model of international order: several major centers of power, Russia as one of them, and the international system understood as a process of negotiating interests among major powers.

A model may suggest that this began in 2007, but that would be an error.


4. Is Russia more often described as a victim, a leader, or a defender?


The image of Russia as a victim is based on themes such as humiliation during the 1990s, NATO enlargement, the disregard of Russian interests, Western double standards, color revolutions, sanctions, and the encirclement of Russia.

The image of Russia as a defender translates these grievances into action: Russia is supposed to protect itself, Russians, the Russian world, historical memory, and traditional values.

At the same time, the triumphalist component should not be overlooked: Russia is portrayed as the victor of the Second World War, a nuclear power, a permanent member of the UN Security Council, and one of the poles of the world. This element is used to justify Russia’s claim to a special position in international affairs.

After 2022, the paradox becomes particularly clear: an offensive war is presented as another defensive war against NATO, neo-Nazism, and alleged attempts to destroy Russia. OSW identifies this mechanism particularly well.

The best answer is that these images are interconnected. They appear contradictory, but in fact they complement one another.


5. How does the narrative toward Poland differ from that toward Germany?


Poland becomes, in Putin’s discourse, primarily a conflict-oriented state: a political opponent of Russia, a supporter of NATO enlargement, a Russophobic country, and, over time, also an object of aggressive historical politics.

The escalation in 2019–2020 is particularly important, when Putin repeatedly accuses Poland of sharing responsibility for the crisis that led to the Second World War, emphasizes antisemitism in the Second Polish Republic, and defends the Soviet interpretation of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact.

Germany is portrayed much more ambivalently. History obviously provides the category of Nazi aggressor, but post-war reconciliation simultaneously makes it possible to portray Germany as a special European economic and political partner.

An important semantic difference is that Poland is often treated as an object or a problem, whereas Germany is portrayed as a potential interlocutor that should be separated from the United States and from the more confrontational part of the West.

After 2022, this difference naturally becomes smaller, because Germany is increasingly incorporated into the category of the collective West.

Poland = opponent / Russophobia / NATO / historical dispute.
Germany = historical enemy, but at the same time a privileged partner and a potentially autonomous European center of power.


6. How does the tone toward the USA compare with that toward the European Union?

Here, I would expect a clearly more negative portrayal of the United States.

In Putin’s discourse, the United States is the main driving force behind unipolarity, NATO enlargement, unilateralism, military interventions, color revolutions, and attempts to impose rules on other states.

For much of Putin’s rule, Europe is treated more ambivalently: it is part of the West, but at the same time an economic partner, a culturally close region, and a potential independent pole in the international system.

A shift becomes particularly visible around 2013–2014, when the EU, partly because of sanctions, increasingly begins to be portrayed as a threat. After 2022, the United States, NATO, and the EU are increasingly merged into the broader category of the collective West, although the United States continues to be portrayed primarily as its leading center.

In short: the United States should be portrayed considerably more negatively than the EU, while the difference between the two should decrease over time.
